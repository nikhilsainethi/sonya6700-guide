Chapter 01

Three settings
make every photo

A photo is just collected light. Three controls decide how much gets collected, and each one changes the look of the picture as a side effect. Learn the side effects and you have learned photography.

f/

Aperture

The size of the hole in the lens.

A wide hole lets in lots of light and melts the background into blur. A small hole lets in little light and keeps everything sharp, near to far.

The catch: the numbers run backwards. f/2.8 is a big hole. f/16 is a tiny one.

1/

Shutter speed

How long the camera looks.

A short look (1/1000 of a second) freezes a skier mid-jump. A long look (1/15) lets in more light but smears anything that moves, including your hands.

Safe floors: 1/125 for still things, 1/250 for people, 1/1000 for action.

ISO

ISO

How much the camera amplifies what it got.

ISO 100 is the cleanest picture. Raising it brightens the photo without needing more light, but adds speckly grain called noise.

On your a6700: up to about 3200 looks clean, 6400 is fine, beyond that is for emergencies.

The trade is always the same

Photographers measure light in stops. One stop is a doubling or a halving. Every click of the sliders above is one stop, which is why they can cancel each other out: open the aperture one stop, speed the shutter up one stop, and the brightness lands exactly where it was. Only the look has changed.

You will rarely set all three yourself. On your camera's mode dial, A lets you choose the aperture and the camera works out the rest. Start there.

See which dials do this on the camera →

A
Aperture priority. You pick the blur, the camera picks the shutter. Leave the dial here for your first month.
S
Shutter priority. You pick the speed, the camera picks the aperture. For action and for falling snow.
M
Manual. You pick both. With ISO on Auto it is friendlier than it sounds.
P
Program. The camera picks both, you can still nudge brightness. Handing-it-to-a-stranger mode.

Worth knowing

The a6700's sensor is a size called APS-C, a little smaller than the "full-frame" standard that lens numbers are usually quoted against. Chapter 03

Why your snow
comes out grey

Your camera cannot know it is looking at snow. It assumes every scene averages out to a medium grey, sees a frame full of brilliant white, concludes it is far too bright, and darkens it. The fix is one control.

±0.0
−2 darker+3 brighter

  1. Find the ± control. It is the bottom edge of the control wheel on the back. Press it, then turn the wheel. Show me on the camera
  2. Dial in +1 before you take the first frame. Sunny snow often wants +1.3 to +1.7.
  3. Read the graph, not the screen. Push the big hump to the right until it sits near the edge without piling up against it.

Which lens goes up the mountain

Bright, dry day

Sony 18–135

Snow in sunshine is one of the brightest scenes you will ever shoot, so the dimmer aperture costs you nothing. You get wide landscapes and distant skiers from one lens that never has to come off.

Snow falling, dusk, or people

Sigma 18–50

Storm light is dim. f/2.8 keeps your shutter fast enough to freeze flakes, blurs the background behind a face, and the sealed mount is a little more forgiving of damp.

Either way, choose before you leave and don't change lenses outside while it is snowing. An open camera mount and a snowflake is how sensors get spotted.

Keeping the camera alive in the cold

  1. Cold eats batteries

    Expect half the usual life below freezing. Carry a spare in an inside pocket against your body and swap them when one fades. A "dead" cold battery often revives once warm.

  2. Warm rooms are the real danger

    A frozen camera carried into a heated cabin fogs up inside and out. Before you go in, seal it in your camera bag or a zip-lock bag and leave it closed for an hour so it warms slowly.

  3. Brush, don't blow

    Your breath freezes onto glass. Flick snow off with a glove or a dry cloth, and keep the lens hood on: it stops most flakes reaching the front of the lens.

  4. Damp is not the same as wet

    The a6700 is built to resist dust and moisture, not to be soaked. Light dry snow is fine. Wet sleet means a cover, a jacket, or putting it away.

Chapter 04

Twelve settings,
then stop fiddling

The a6700's menu has hundreds of options and you need about a dozen. Set these once, on the sofa, and the camera will stay out of your way. Names are written the way the menu spells them. Tick them off as you go; this page remembers.

0of 12 set

Four drills for your first week

  1. The mug. Put a mug on a table with a window behind it. Shoot it at f/2.8, f/5.6 and f/11. Compare the backgrounds.
  2. The tap. Run a tap. Shoot it at 1/2000 and at 1/15. Water turns from beads into silk.
  3. The dark room. Same indoor scene at ISO 400, 3200 and 12800. Zoom in on the shadows and find your own limit.
  4. The white wall. Fill the frame with a white wall and take one shot. It will be grey. Add +1.3 and shoot again. That is the whole snow chapter in ten seconds.

Chapter 06 · open this one on the mountain

Pocket card

  1. Small f-number, blurry background. Big f-number, everything sharp.
  2. 1/250 for people. 1/1000 for anything that runs, skis or flies.
  3. Raise ISO last, but raise it. Grainy and sharp beats clean and blurred.
  4. Snow in the frame? Dial +1. Then check the graph.
  5. Trust the histogram, not the screen. Screens lie in bright sun.
  6. Focus on the nearest eye. Let the camera's eye detection do it.
  7. Half-press, pause, press. Jabbing the button blurs the shot.
  8. Spare battery in an inside pocket. Warm batteries work.
  9. Bag the camera before going indoors. One hour, closed.
  10. Never change lenses in falling snow.
  11. Hood on, always. It blocks flakes, glare and knocks.
  12. Take the second photo from lower down. It is usually the better one.
